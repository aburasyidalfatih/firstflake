Before we start

A Letter from Josie

Hi, friend.

If you're holding this, you've probably already bought a gold pan. Maybe it's still in the plastic. Maybe you've tried it once, washed a whole afternoon of gravel back into the creek, and gone home with sore knees and nothing to show for it. Both are fine. Everyone starts somewhere, and nobody starts good. I certainly didn't.

Here's what I wish someone had told me sooner: finding gold isn't luck. It's reading. Rivers sort their gravel the same way every single time, and once you know the pattern, you stop digging at random and start digging where the river already put it.

That's what this workbook does. Seven trips. Each one teaches one skill, gives you one rule to remember, and has a page where you write down what actually happened. By Trip 7 you won't be hoping anymore. You'll know where to look, how to get it out, and how to prove it's real.

A couple of honest things before we go. You're not going to get rich. Most of what you'll find is flakes smaller than a sesame seed. But I promise you, the first time one of those flakes sits in the bottom of your pan, glowing in the shade, you will yell. Everyone yells. I've watched grown men in hip waders do it.

My great-grandpa Ezra has been panning since 1939. We all just call him Grandpa, and he'll be popping up in the margins with his two cents. He insisted. (He's 100. You don't argue with a man who's 100.)

Grab your pan. Let's go read a river.

Josie

The science, in one page

Why This Works

Rivers are sorting machines. Learn how they sort.

Gold is heavy. Really heavy. A flake of gold weighs about seven times as much as a grain of ordinary sand the same size. (Gold's density is about 19.3 grams per cubic centimeter. Quartz sand is about 2.65.) That one fact is the whole science of this book.

When a creek floods, it picks up sand, gravel and gold and carries them downstream. The moment the water slows down, it drops what it can't carry anymore, and it drops the heaviest things first. So gold ends up in the same kinds of places, creek after creek:

  1. The inside of bends, where the water slows on the curve
  2. The head of gravel bars, the upstream end where the bar begins
  3. The quiet water behind big boulders, on the downstream side
  4. Cracks and low spots in bedrock, where gold sinks and gets trapped
  5. Streaks of black sand, the heavy minerals that travel with gold

Old-timers learned this by trial and error over whole lifetimes. Geologists call it placer deposition. You're going to learn it in seven trips, by looking first and digging second.

What this workbook is not

  • Not a promise of riches. Recreational panners usually find small flakes. Their real value is in the finding.
  • Not legal advice. Where you can pan depends on your state and the type of land. The Safety & Permission page shows you how to check.
  • Not a shortcut. The skill lives in your hands, and hands learn by doing. That's why every trip ends with a page you fill in.

The Empty Pan Rule

You will have empty pans. Probably whole empty days. Here's the rule for those days:

No restarts. No catching up. No doubling up.
Just do the next trip.

Missed a weekend? Do the next trip. Rained out for a month? Do the next trip. Went to the creek and found nothing? Write it in the log, then do the next trip.

An empty pan isn't a failure. It's information. It tells you where the gold isn't, and in prospecting that's half of knowing where it is. Every real prospector has a notebook full of zeros. The difference is, they kept the notebook.

The log is a map, not a report card.

Your life, your creek

Make It Fit Your Life

The seven trips are the default. Here's how to bend them without breaking them.

No creek nearby?

Buy a bag of paydirt (real gold-bearing gravel sold for practice) and do Trips 1, 4 and 5 at home in a tub. Many states also have public panning areas and pay-to-pan spots where beginners are welcome. Save Trips 2, 3, 6 and 7 for when you can get to real water.

Bringing kids?

Give every kid a job: sieve captain, BB counter, flake spotter. Keep them in water below the knee and away from anything fast. Cold water tires small bodies quickly, so plan short sessions and pack dry socks. A life jacket for anyone small is never overkill.

Knees, back or balance not what they were?

You don't have to kneel in the creek. Sit on a bucket with a lid, work from the bank, and let a classifier do the heavy lifting. A long-handled scoop saves your back. Panning in a tub on a picnic table is still panning.

Only have one free day?

Start with the One-Day Plan at the back of this book. Then come back and do the trips properly once you're hooked. You will be.

Confused about the rules where you live?

Call your local Bureau of Land Management (BLM) field office or Forest Service ranger district. They answer these questions every day, and asking them is exactly what experienced prospectors do. It's the smart move, not the beginner move.

No printer?

Copy the log tables into any notebook. A $2 composition book in a zip-top bag is a perfectly good field log.

Safety & Permission Basics

Stay safe

  • Cold water is the real danger. Mountain creeks can numb your hands in minutes, even in July. Take breaks, wear gloves, and bring dry clothes.
  • Check the weather upstream, not just where you're standing. Rain in the mountains can raise a creek fast. If the water rises or turns muddy, get out.
  • Never enter old mine tunnels or shafts. They collapse and can hold bad air. Stay out, stay alive.
  • Don't dig into creek banks or under overhangs. They can come down on you.
  • Tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back. Better yet, bring a friend.
  • Never use mercury. Old-timers did. It poisons you and the river, and you don't need it.

Stay legal (a general guide, not legal advice)

  • Private land: you need the owner's permission. Always.
  • National parks: prospecting and collecting are generally not allowed.
  • BLM land and national forests: casual panning is often allowed, but not on ground someone has already claimed. An active mining claim gives the claim holder the mineral rights, even on public land.
  • Checking for claims: the BLM's online Mineral & Land Records System (MLRS, mlrs.blm.gov) lets you look up mining claims, and your local BLM office can help you read it.
  • State rules vary. Some states limit tools, seasons, or digging in fish habitat. Check your state's natural resources or fish and wildlife agency before you go.
  • Leave it better than you found it. Fill your holes, put rocks back, pack out your trash. That's how panning stays allowed.

Before Trip 1 · The step most beginners skip

Choose Your Creek

You can't pan gold out of a creek that never had any.

Here's the honest truth about why most beginners quit: their technique was fine, but they panned the nearest pretty creek, and that creek never had gold in it. The pan only finds what's there. So before you buy gas, spend one evening finding a creek with a gold history that you're allowed to pan.

Five places to look tonight

  1. Public panning areas. Many states and federal agencies set aside creeks just for recreational panning. These are the easiest start: known gold, no claims. Search "[your state] public gold panning area" and look for results from .gov sites.
  2. Your state geological survey. Most western and Appalachian states publish free gold maps, mining-district lists, or a "where to pan" page.
  3. The USGS Mineral Resources Data System (mrdata.usgs.gov). Its map shows old gold mines and prospects. Old placer mines on or near public land are a strong sign.
  4. Historic mining districts. Towns with names like Gold Hill, Placerville or Nugget Creek earned them. Old mining history means the river had gold, and floods keep bringing more down.
  5. People. A local prospecting club or GPAA chapter, a rock shop, or the BLM field office. Prospectors are famously cagey about their spots, but happy to point a beginner at a public one.

Then check three things

  • Is it public, and open? Not a national park, not private land without permission.
  • Is it claimed? Look it up in MLRS, or call the BLM office.
  • What are the rules? Tools allowed, seasons, and fish-habitat limits for that stream.
Ezra says: "I never once found gold in a creek nobody else had found gold in first. Follow the old-timers."

Before Trip 1 · Picture it

How to Hold a Gold Pan

Three motions. That's all panning is.

Know your pan

Most beginner pans are plastic, 10 to 14 inches across, and green or black so gold shows up. One side has riffles, the ridges that catch heavy material. Keep the riffles on the side away from you when you wash, and on the low side of the pan.

Level, fully underwater,
hard side-to-side, 20 sec
Riffles on the far side,
lip just under the surface
Gentle forward-and-back.
Level and shake every few swishes

The rhythm

Shake, tilt, wash. Shake, tilt, wash.

The shake sends heavy things down. The tilt and wash carry light things out. If you only wash and never shake, gold climbs up and leaves with the sand. That's the number one beginner mistake, and the Tub Rehearsal fixes it.

Do

  • Keep the pan underwater while you shake
  • Break up every clay clump with your fingers
  • Go slower as the pan gets lighter

Don't

  • Tip the pan steeply, it dumps everything
  • Stir it like soup
  • Rush the last few spoonfuls

Trip 1 of 7

The Tub Rehearsal

The Tub Rule: Lose it at home, so you never lose it in the river.

Where: your backyard, garage or bathtub  ·  Time: about 45 minutes

Why this trip comes first

Every beginner I've ever taught makes the same mistake. They drive two hours to a creek, pan like they're stirring soup, and wash their gold straight back into the water without ever seeing it. You can't learn a new motion and read a river at the same time. So today, the river comes to you.

Here's the trick: gold is about seven times heavier than the sand and gravel around it. Anything heavy enough will behave the same way in your pan. So we practice with something you can count.

What to bring

  • Your gold pan (the riffles are the ridges along one side)
  • A plastic tub or bin, wider than your pan, filled with water
  • Two big scoops of gravel and sand from your yard or a garden store
  • 10 steel BBs. These are your "gold." Skip lead sinkers, especially if kids are helping.

At the tub: step by step

  1. Mix your 10 BBs into the gravel, then fill your pan about three-quarters full.
  2. Sink the whole pan under the water. Break up any clumps with your fingers until everything is loose and muddy.
  3. Shake the pan hard, side to side, still under the water, for about 20 seconds. This is the part everyone rushes. Don't. The shaking is what sends heavy things to the bottom.
  4. Pick out the big rocks by hand. Glance at each one, then toss it.
  5. Tilt the pan forward, riffles on the far side, so the edge dips just under the surface. Swish gently forward and back so the water carries the top layer out over the edge.
  6. Every few swishes, level the pan and shake again. Shake, tilt, wash. Shake, tilt, wash.
  7. When you're down to a few spoonfuls, swirl slowly and count your BBs.

Level up: once you can find all 10, flatten 3 BBs with a hammer. Flat flakes are harder to keep, just like real gold.

Trip 2 of 7

Read the River

The Inside Bend Rule: Gold stops where the water gets lazy.

Where: a creek with a gold history where you're allowed to pan  ·  Time: about 2 hours

Why we look before we dig

Today you'll spend more time looking than digging. That will feel wrong. Do it anyway.

The river has already done the hard work of sorting its gravel. Your job is to read where it put the heavy stuff, and you can do most of that reading from the bank before your boots even get wet.

What to bring

  • Your gold pan and a small shovel or garden trowel
  • A bucket
  • This workbook and a pencil
  • Water shoes or boots, snacks, and plenty of water

At the creek: step by step

  1. Walk the creek for 15 minutes before you touch a shovel. Go upstream and down. Just look.
  2. Find the bends. On each one, look at the inside curve, the side with the gravel bar. That's where the water slows.
  3. Find the head of each gravel bar, the upstream end where the bar begins. Heavy material drops there first.
  4. Find the big boulders and look at the quiet water right behind them, on the downstream side.
  5. Look for dark streaks in the gravel. That's black sand, and gold travels with it.
  6. Look for bedrock showing at the edges of the creek and note any cracks. You'll come back for them on Trip 3.
  7. Pick your 3 best spots, sketch them on the next page, and take one pan from each with your Tub Rehearsal motion.
Also look for: moss and tree roots at the waterline that flood every spring (they trap fine gold), and old diggings, rock piles and tailings, which tell you someone found gold here before you.

Trip 3 of 7

Down to Bedrock

The Bedrock Rule: Gold sinks until it can't.

Where: exposed bedrock at the edge of your creek  ·  Time: 2 to 3 hours

Why bedrock matters

Gold is so heavy that, over years of floods, it works its way down through the gravel until it hits solid rock. Then it has nowhere left to go. It settles into cracks, dips and potholes in the bedrock and stays there.

That's why old-timers always said to get down to bedrock. Where bedrock shows at the edge of a creek, you can reach those cracks with a screwdriver instead of a backhoe.

What to bring

  • A crevice tool (a long flathead screwdriver or an old spoon works fine)
  • A small stiff brush, your pan and a bucket
  • Work gloves, and knee pads or a folded towel

At the creek: step by step

  1. Find bedrock at the water's edge, ideally on an inside bend or where the creek has run over it.
  2. Look for cracks that run across the current, not along it. Cross-cracks trap more.
  3. Clear away the loose gravel sitting on top.
  4. Scrape the crack out with your crevice tool, all the way to the bottom. The packed clay and fine material at the very bottom is what you want.
  5. Brush everything from the crack into your pan.
  6. Pan it with your Tub Rehearsal motion. Log every crack, including the empty ones.
  7. Leave the spot tidy and put loose rocks back.
Stay safe: never pry rock out of a bank or from under an overhang, and stay off slick, mossy bedrock in fast water.

Well now. If you're reading this, you've done three trips, and I'd wager at least one of them sent you home with an empty pan and a sore back.

Good. That means you're doing it right.

Trip 4 of 7

Classify & Concentrate

The Bucket Rule: Move more dirt, not faster hands.

Where: your best spot from Trips 2 and 3  ·  Time: 2 to 3 hours

Why a bucket beats a pan

One pan is a sample. A bucket is a chance. The more material you move from a good spot, the better your odds. But panning big rocks is slow, and it's how beginners lose gold.

A classifier (a screen that sits on top of a bucket) fixes that. It sifts out the rocks so you only pan the fine, heavy material. And today you won't finish every pan. You'll stop at the heavy stuff, called concentrates, and save it for Trip 5.

What to bring

  • A classifier screen that fits a 5-gallon bucket. Start with 1/2 inch mesh. A 1/8 inch screen is great for the last step.
  • Two 5-gallon buckets, a shovel and your pan
  • A small jar with a lid for your concentrates

At the creek: step by step

  1. Go to your best spot from Trip 2 or 3 and fill one bucket with material. Dig down toward bedrock if you can.
  2. Set the classifier on the empty bucket and add a few scoops of material.
  3. Pour creek water through it, or dunk and shake it in the creek, so the fines fall through into the bucket.
  4. Glance at the rocks left on the screen. Any nuggets? It happens. Then toss them.
  5. Pan the fines in batches. Stop each pan when you're down to a thin layer of dark, heavy material.
  6. Tip those concentrates into your jar. Don't finish them here.
  7. Time yourself. How many pans can you do in an hour?

Trip 5 of 7

Black Sand School

The Black Sand Rule: Where there's black sand, gold isn't far.

Where: at home, in a tub  ·  Time: about 1 hour

Why black sand is your best clue

Black sand is mostly heavy iron minerals like magnetite. It isn't as heavy as gold, but it's heavier than almost everything else in the creek, so it travels with gold and settles in the same places. That's why it ends up at the bottom of your pan, and why it's the best clue you have. Today you'll learn to separate gold from black sand gently, at home, where nothing can wash away.

What to bring

  • Your concentrates jar from Trip 4
  • A tub of water and your pan
  • A strong magnet sealed inside a zip-top bag
  • A snuffer bottle (a small squeeze bottle with a tube)
  • A small glass vial filled with water

At the tub: step by step

  1. Pan a small batch of concentrates in the tub, gently, until mostly black sand is left.
  2. Leave a little water in the pan and tilt it slightly toward you.
  3. Swirl slowly in a small circle and watch the back edge of the black sand. Gold lags behind and shows up as a little yellow tail.
  4. Suck the flakes up with the snuffer bottle and squirt them into your vial.
  5. Hold the bagged magnet just above the black sand to lift out the magnetite. Gold isn't magnetic, so it stays behind. Check what's left.
  6. Save the leftover black sand in a jar. Fine gold hides in it, and you can re-pan it on any rainy day.

Trip 6 of 7

The Sample Line

The Sample Rule: One pan is a guess. Ten pans are a map.

Where: one inside bend or gravel bar on your creek  ·  Time: 2 to 3 hours

Why we test in a pattern

Gold isn't sprinkled evenly. It collects in streaks and pockets, sometimes along a line the old-timers called the paystreak. The only way to find it is to test in a pattern and write everything down.

Prospectors count "colors," which just means the number of gold specks in one full pan. Professional geologists sample the same way, only with a drill instead of a pan.

What to bring

  • Pan, shovel, bucket and classifier
  • This workbook and a pencil
  • 6 to 8 small flags or marked rocks to label your spots

At the creek: step by step

  1. Choose one inside bend or gravel bar you sketched on Trip 2.
  2. Mark 6 to 8 spots about 10 to 20 steps apart, from the head of the bar toward the tail, plus any bedrock cracks nearby.
  3. At each spot, dig to the same depth and fill your pan the same amount. Fair tests only.
  4. Pan each one all the way down and count the colors.
  5. Write the count on your sketch and in the log right away. Don't trust your memory.
  6. Look at the numbers. Is there a line where the counts go up? That's your streak.
  7. Circle your best spot. That's where you'll go on Trip 7.

Trip 7 of 7

First Flake Day

The Stay Rule: Go back to your best spot, and stay there.

Where: your circled spot from Trip 6  ·  Time: half a day

Why today is different

Beginners hop around the creek chasing the next spot. Prospectors who find gold do the opposite. Once your sample line shows you where the numbers go up, you stay and work it.

Dig to bedrock, classify, concentrate, and finish carefully. Everything you've learned comes together today.

What to bring

  • Everything: pan, shovel, classifier, two buckets, crevice tool
  • Concentrates jar, snuffer bottle, vial, and the magnet in its bag
  • A sewing pin for the tests, and your phone for a photo

At the creek: step by step

  1. Go to your best spot from Trip 6.
  2. Dig down toward bedrock and clean out any cracks you find.
  3. Classify and concentrate at least one full bucket.
  4. Finish your concentrates gently, at the creek or back home in the tub.
  5. Snuff every flake into your vial.
  6. Run the three tests on the next page.
  7. Take a photo of your vial. You earned it.

If your best spot comes up empty today

  • Go deeper first. Most "dead" spots are surface-only. Get closer to bedrock or the hard-packed layer.
  • Work upstream along the streak. Gold in your sample line came from somewhere above it.
  • Re-pan your tailings. Run your leftover material through once more, slowly. Beginners lose more than they think.
  • Check your black sand jar. Fine gold hides there. Finish it at home before you call it a zero.
  • Still nothing? Run a new sample line on the next bend. Trip 7 isn't one day. It's "stay until you find it."

After your first flake

What to Do With Your Gold

Everyone asks two questions after the yell: what's it worth, and what do I do with it?

Keep it safe

  • Store flakes in a glass vial filled with water. Water magnifies the gold and keeps tiny flakes from sticking to the glass or blowing away.
  • Label the vial with the creek and date. In ten years that label is the best part.
  • Rinse out stray black sand with a snuffer bottle. A drop of dish soap stops flakes from floating.

What's it worth?

Gold is priced per troy ounce, which is 31.1 grams. To estimate your value: grams × (today's gold price ÷ 31.1).

What you've gotRough weightValue at $4,000 an ounce
One small flakeabout 0.001 to 0.005 groughly 15 to 65 cents
A pickerabout 0.05 groughly $6
A good vial after a seasonabout 0.5 to 1 groughly $65 to $130
One full gram1 groughly $129

Weights vary a lot with flake size and thickness. Treat these as ballpark numbers, and check the day's gold price.

Weigh it

A jeweler's scale that reads to 0.001 gram costs about $20 to $40. Dry the gold first, and weigh it on a small piece of paper.

Should you sell it?

  • Small amounts: refiners and pawn shops usually pay well under the gold price for small lots, if they'll buy at all. Most hobbyists keep their flakes.
  • Nuggets and pretty pieces: collectors often pay more than the gold price for natural nuggets with character. Don't melt them.
  • Keep it legal: in some places, gold from certain public lands is for personal, non-commercial use only. Check before you sell.

Before You Close This Book

Josie and Ezra are fictional characters created with the help of AI. The people and their stories are invented. The geology, techniques and safety advice are real and were written to be accurate, but please double-check anything that matters with an official source.

This is not legal advice. Rules about where and how you can prospect vary by state, land type and season, and they change. Before you dig, check with the landowner, your local Bureau of Land Management field office, the Forest Service ranger district, or your state's natural resources or fish and wildlife agency.

This is not financial advice. Recreational panners usually find small amounts of gold. Gold values in this book are rough examples. Do this for the creek, the skill and the yell, not for a paycheck.

Rivers can be dangerous. Cold water, fast currents, rising water and old mine workings can hurt or kill. You're responsible for your own safety and for anyone you bring along, especially kids. If in doubt, stay out.

If you're ever lost or hurt outdoors, call 911. Many remote areas have spotty signal, so tell someone your plan before you go.
